Inquiry, Conversation, Argument · Winter 2026

What Kind of Future Does Invention Imagine?

University of Chicago · Writing Program · WRIT 10100-2 · T/Th 9:30–10:50, Wieboldt 230

Invention is never just a technical breakthrough: it's a cultural event, a story about the future, a way of shaping relationships and distributing power. In this section of Inquiry, Conversation, Argument, we'll explore invention as a rhetorical and analytical problem: How do we talk about innovation? Who gets to invent, and who is left out? What kinds of futures do our inventions assume or erase?

This course challenges us to slow down our thinking and write about invention as a site of social complexity. We'll treat familiar objects—like smartphones, biometric sensors, or doorbell cameras—as entry points into systems of meaning, identity, and control. Readings will span science writing, critical design theory, speculative fiction, cultural critique, and empirical research. We'll reckon seriously with the invention that defines our present moment—artificial intelligence—not to deliver a verdict about it, but to understand it from the inside out, with honesty and care.

Our goal isn't just clarity, but depth: to build the kind of writing that helps us think harder, argue better, and see more clearly how invention shapes the world we live in—and whether there's one we might choose to make instead.

Major assignments

Textual Analysis Exercise — 10%

Practice the core skills we'll use all quarter: reading closely, identifying stakes, and making interpretive claims. You'll choose a short passage from an early course reading and write an analysis that shows how it works—what it argues, what it assumes, and why it matters.

Analytic Essay — 20%

Develop an independent interpretive position derived from analysis of an intellectually diverse range of texts. By synthesizing ideas from our readings and developing an argument of your own, you'll contribute to an ongoing conversation about a specific puzzle, problem, or question related to our course themes.

Implosion Prospectus — 30%

Propose and explore a question you want to consider in depth. You'll conduct preliminary research, analyze sources, and make an argument about the significance and stakes of your question—considering disciplinary perspectives and why this question matters for you and for others.

Prospectus Seed — scaffolding

A short piece of exploratory writing due Week 5. Identify an object, technology, or system you might want to implode, describe it in a paragraph, and write your first attempt at a question about it. This is thinking on the page, not a draft—there are no wrong answers, only more and less interesting ones.

Writer's Memo — scaffolding

A one-page reflection due Week 8, after you've received feedback on Draft 2. Answer four questions: What is your argument right now, in your own words? What does your feedback tell you needs to change? What's the one thing you're going to do differently in the final draft? And—just as importantly—what's the one thing you're protecting?

Participation and Engagement — 40%

Speaking up in class sessions, peer workshops, and in-class activities; completing in-class writing projects and written peer review; and engaging with all scaffolding assignments.

On AI and this course

This course is a protected space for a specific kind of thinking—and that protection is the point.

It's not that AI tools have no value, or that using them makes you a bad student, or that I haven't thought carefully about what they can and can't do. I have, and I think about it a lot. It's that this course is designed to give you ten weeks of something increasingly rare: time to encounter your own thinking more fully, to find out what you actually believe by writing your way toward it, to stay with difficulty long enough for something serious to emerge. AI use at any stage of that process forecloses exactly the experience the course is built to provide. Not because the output would be bad. Because the thinking that didn't happen is the thing you came here for.

So I'm asking you to treat this course the way you might treat any protected space: not as a restriction imposed on you, but as a condition you're choosing to accept because of what it makes possible.

There is one deliberate exception. In Week 3 we will do a structured in-class exercise in which you use an AI tool under specific conditions and reflect carefully on the experience. This is not a loophole—it's part of the argument. You'll write something on your own, bring it to an AI, and then reckon honestly with what happened: What did it take up? What did it offer that you didn't? What did you refuse? What are you now answerable for?

Outside of that exercise, the prohibition is total. Use search engines to find sources, but go to the sources themselves—don't rely on AI-generated summaries or overviews as substitutes for reading. Tools that check spelling and basic grammar are fine; tools that rewrite, suggest, or generate language—including Grammarly's advanced features—are not.

But I'd rather you come to me with a question or a struggle than make a decision in the dark. I'm not trying to catch you. I'm trying to give you something worth protecting.

Policies

Attendance

Attendance at all class sessions is expected. After three missed classes, you risk failing the course. Because this course is run as a seminar, consistent attendance is essential.

Late work

All students receive a one-day, no-questions-asked extension for ONE assignment, provided it is not part of a peer-review activity. Email me before the due date to use it. The final Prospectus draft must be turned in on time.

Devices

Class time is device-free except in rare, explicitly stated cases. Please take notes by hand and bring hard copies of readings. If you have accommodations through Student Disability Services, let me know.

Contact

Office hours: T 11:00–12:00 & Th 2:15–3:15, Gates-Blake 213, and by appointment. Email: hutch@uchicago.edu. I aim to respond within 24 hours.
